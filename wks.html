Week 1 WKS Reflection

Introducing Digital Practices

This week introduced the idea that technology and society shape each other. I found this more useful than simply asking whether AI makes us smarter or lazier. As an international student, I can see why AI translation feels reassuring when a reading is difficult. However, an easy summary might also hide a point I need to think through myself. This made me realise that saving time and understanding something are not always the same. I want to use AI to help me ask better questions, while still checking the original text.

Week 2 WKS Reflection

Creating and Hosting a Website

This was my first time making a website and writing code, so I wasn't sure where to start. Following Dr Holly step by step really helped, I could try each change and see what it did. I learned how to publish a site through GitHub Pages or CampusPress, and was happy to see my own page online. It's still a simple draft, but making changes feels less intimidating now.

What we learned

Create an index.html file with a title, headings and paragraphs.
link a stylesheet to change colours, fonts and spacing.
build pages using themes and a content management system.
upload website files to a repository and publish them online.

Week 3 WKS Reflection

Web scraping

In this workshop, I used a downloaded scraping tool to collect data from BBC iPlayer. The AI scraper sometimes got things wrong, so I had to try again and check what it produced. I learned that getting a CSV file is only the start: my export included blank cells and button text such as "View all", which would need cleaning before analysis.

I collected programme listings rather than personal details, but the task still made me think about copyright, respecting the website's rules and avoiding too many requests.

Week 4 WKS Reflection

Data practice

I found it hard to decide how broad our topic should be. We started with AI and education, then narrowed it to university students' use of generative AI. We settled on two questions: how often do students use AI, and what do they mainly use it for? This made planning the data collection feel more manageable. I also realised that choosing numerical variables means deciding what counts as a useful answer. Categories can make data easier to compare, but they may leave out some of the reasons behind students' choices.

Week 5 WKS Reflection

Data Visualisation

This week, I found it tricky to group students' different uses of AI without losing detail. Our questions about frequency and purpose seemed simple, but the categories we chose could affect the story. Collecting data to improve study support would also mean asking different questions from investigating cheating. I became more aware of privacy and the risk of making students feel judged. For the visualisation, the challenge was keeping it clear without suggesting that our data represented every student. I learned to think about who might be missing before focusing on how a chart looks.

Week 6 WKS Reflection

Identity, Algorithmic Identity and Representation

I manually classified the latest 15 posts from 32 friends using Sumpter's matrix. It was a bit boring, and some posts mixed several topics or involved hobbies that did not fit the categories. The table and graph made patterns easier to see, but I would not recognise a whole person from these counts. Comparing platform inputs and outputs also made me question what an advertising profile leaves out. It was not clear how the advertising labels were chosen. This helped me understand algorithmic identity as a partial picture shaped by the system, rather than a complete description of someone.

Week 7 WKS Reflection

Reflective Pause

Revisiting Sumpter's matrix made me question what our neat table actually showed. Collecting posts felt repetitive, but deciding where they belonged was harder. A post could mix art, daily life and personal feelings. Putting it in one category made comparison easier while hiding that mixture. Reposts might show what friends enjoy sharing, but they cannot tell me why it matters to them. If I repeated the task, I would allow multiple categories and ask friends how they understood their own posts.

The AI detection task raised a similar problem. We judged five excerpts through formatting, references, tone and academic style. I realised that features I associate with good student writing could also look "AI-like" to me. Learning that all the excerpts came from student work graded at least 2:1 challenged my confidence. This did not establish how each text was produced, so assigning an "AI percentage" still felt unjustified. Our guesses were not a test of detection software, but they showed how easily assumptions can become labels. As someone writing in a second language, I found this unsettling: practising clear, formal English could make my writing look suspicious under the same rules.

Crawford's point about classification as world-making helped connect both activities (2021, p. 121). Categories shape the people or texts we think we see. The consequences also differ: simplifying a friend's interests is one thing, while questioning a student's honesty can affect their education. I now think good academic practice needs more attention to the writing process: checking sources, keeping drafts and explaining any AI assistance. Before accepting a label, I would now ask how it was made and whose judgement it reflects.

Week 8 WKS Reflection

Digital Ecologies in Practice

Smell stood out during our visit to the garden and café. We noticed coffee, a tea-like scent from berries around campus, and how the buildings felt. We recorded these experiences in a hand-drawn sensory map, with each group member drawing their own part. I liked having room to express an impression through drawing. Yet putting our contributions together made me wonder whether the same marks meant the same thing to everyone. A shared page could make our different experiences look more consistent than they were.

This connects with Searle et al.'s discussion of mediation as transformation (2024, pp. 9-11). Choosing colours, shapes and words shaped how someone else might imagine the place. The berries, coffee, buildings and our bodies all contributed to the experience we tried to represent. A scan could make our map easy to share online, but viewers would still need to imagine the smells and physical sensations. Adding descriptions might help, although comparing a scent to tea also depends on personal memory and familiarity.

The activity extended our earlier questions about what counts as data. Sensations gave us something valuable to work with, while also making the limits of our account clearer. Our map reflected particular people at a particular time. It could miss experiences of someone who smells less strongly or moves through campus differently. Next time, I would ask each person to explain their marks, note the conditions and compare the route on another day. For me, countermapping becomes useful when it makes these differences visible and questions whose experience a map usually presents.
